Case 1 of 6 · Valuing

“What is this residential plot in a JDA scheme worth?”

The short answer

Start from a plot sold in the same scheme in the same year, adjust for size, and quote a range of about 14% either way, not one figure.

A client owns a residential plot in a JDA scheme, not on a corner. It is 162 sq m (194 sq yd), the middle size of plot JDA sold at its auctions in 2025-26. You have the rate a 100-150 sq m (120-179 sq yd) residential plot fetched at JDA's auction in the same scheme, in the same year. What is his plot worth?

The figure to keep in mind

At JDA's auctions, a plot compared with others sold in the same scheme, for the same use and in the same year, landed within about 14.2% of their usual rate, either way, for 8 plots in 10.

The worked example

For the sum I use an example rate: ₹43,625 per sq m (₹36,476 per sq yd), the middle winning rate at JDA's auctions in 2025 (half the plots sold above it, half below). It is only an example. Put in the same-scheme rate you actually have.

How far one comparable can be trusted

Around the comparable's rate, 8 in 10 plots in the same scheme and year sold within the narrow band. Carry the rate to another year and the band opens out.

JDA's post-auction results, read to 10 November 2026; my sums.

Every step of the sum 6 steps

  1. 01

    Start from a plot sold in the same scheme, for the same use, in the same year

    The best comparable is a residential plot JDA auctioned in the same scheme in the same year. Here I use ₹43,625 per sq m as an example. A plot from another scheme, or another year, is a much weaker guide (step five).

  2. 02

    Adjust for size

    In the same scheme and year, a 150-200 sq m (179-239 sq yd) residential plot fetched −3.5% per sq m against a 100-150 sq m one. So the rate for your client's plot is ₹42,098 per sq m. For a house plot, size hardly moves the rate: from 25-100 sq m to 300-500 sq m, plots sat between −5.4% and −0.2% of the same base.

    For analyststhe size step could be as low as −7.2% or as high as +0.4% (a 95% range), so it may be no step at all.

  3. 03

    Multiply by the plot's size

    ₹42,098 per sq m × 162 sq m = ₹68.20 lakh.

  4. 04

    Turn the figure into a range

    Inside one scheme, one use and one year, 8 in 10 plots sold within about 14.2% of the scheme's usual rate. So the honest answer is ₹58.55 lakh to ₹77.85 lakh, not ₹68.20 lakh.

    For analysts14.2% is half the width of the middle 80% of plots once size and corner are allowed for, measured in 371 scheme, use and year groups with five or more auctions each (5,973 auctions). Before allowing for size and corners, the dearest tenth of plots in a group sold for about 1.23 times the cheapest tenth.

  5. 05

    If your comparable is from another year, do not carry it forward by hand

    I tested this on 2026. I hid every 2026 auction, guessed each plot from its own scheme's sales in the years before, then compared the guess with what it fetched. For 8 plots in 10, the price actually paid ran from about 28% below the guess to about 39% above it (−28.0% to +38.6%). So a comparable from another year is a rough guide, nothing more. Use the comparable adjuster for the size and corner steps, then say in the report that the rate is from another year and quote this wider range around it.

  6. 06

    Add the cost of buying on top

    The buyer also pays stamp duty, two surcharges and the registration fee. For a man buying alone, on a value above ₹10 lakh, that comes to 8.98% of the value. The cost calculator does this on any value and for any buyer.

What it does and does not tell you

What it tells you

  • What a plot like your client's fetched at JDA's auctions, with an honest range around it.
  • That for a house plot in a JDA scheme, size hardly changes the rate per sq m.

What it does not tell you

  • What the plot would fetch on the open market. The band describes JDA's auctions: one seller's plots, in its own schemes.
  • Anything for a corner plot. This sum is for a plot not on a corner.
  • How sure an old comparable is beyond one test: the wider band comes from one year, 2026, a year of fast rises.
  • Whether bids are anchored: JDA sets its auctions per sq m, which may pull bids towards its own figure.
  • Where prices go next.

What to do this week

  1. Use a comparable from the same scheme, the same use and the same year. If you only have one from another year, say so in the report and widen the range.
  2. Quote the value as a range of about 14% either way, not as one figure. Put the range in the summary, not only in the appendix.
  3. Write "JDA auction rate" in the report, never "market rate". JDA's auctions are one government seller's plots in its own schemes.

The tests behind this

The tests behind this, for analysts

One plain sentence per test, the technical name in brackets.

  • The size step compares each plot only with others sold in the same scheme and year, allowing for use, corner, month and the kind of auction (a regression with scheme and year effects, errors clustered by scheme; 4,070 residential auctions).
  • The band measures how far plots sold from their own scheme's usual rate, once size and corner are allowed for (the spread of residuals in 371 scheme, use and year groups).
  • The other-year band hides all of 2026 and guesses each plot from its own scheme's sales in the years before, then sets the guess against the price paid (an out-of-sample holdout of the scheme model on 338 auctions; the 10th and 90th percentiles of price paid over guess).
  • Each of these was recomputed by separately written code before it went on the site.

The findings behind this case

  • A comparable from the same scheme and year is good to about 14% either way.
  • A big commercial (shop) plot fetches less per sq m; a big house plot barely does.
